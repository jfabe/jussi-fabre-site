Why this matters

Who decides what an agent may touch?

Organisations want AI agents in operations, on the bridge and in the control room. Nobody wants an agent with unlimited access to a control system. The question is not new: industrial security answered it years ago by grouping systems into zones and controlling every connection between them.

These prototypes come from the overlap of 25+ years in OT/IT security and critical infrastructure with a Master Mariner degree and deck officer sea service. The focus is the architecture and the rules around AI, the part where industrial AI projects succeed or fail.

Prototype 01 — Cyber

Conduit

An agentic AI security gateway based on IEC 62443. Conduit sits between AI agents and the systems they call through APIs, and decides what each agent is allowed to do. Systems are grouped into security zones, and every agent action passes through a controlled conduit.

IEC 62443Agentic AIWorking prototype

What it does

  • Places agents and target systems into IEC 62443-style zones
  • Routes every agent action through a policy-controlled conduit
  • Allows, restricts or blocks actions according to zone and agent role
  • Records what agents attempted and what was permitted
Why it mattersA familiar OT security model sets clear, auditable limits on what AI agents can reach, before they get anywhere near a control system.
SIMPLIFIED VIEW — agents reach systems only through the conduit; writes into the OT zone are blocked by policy.
Prototype 02 — Sea

Fathom

A vessel self-assessment of onboard AI-agent exposure against IACS UR E26. Fathom shows a ship owner or operator how ready a vessel is for AI agents on board, using the five E26 domains: Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond and Recover.

IACS UR E26MaritimeWorking prototype

What it does

  • Scores each E26 domain in maturity bands: Not ready, Developing, Aligned
  • Radar view of strong and weak areas at a glance
  • Inventory of onboard AI agents with security-zone classification
  • Exports a readiness brief for the fleet or management team
  • Runs fully in the browser; no vessel data leaves the device
Why it mattersE26 applies to new ships, and AI is arriving on board faster than guidance for it. Fathom connects the two in a format a superintendent or master can use, built with deck officer experience behind it.

Applied to data centres

From prototype to the data centre build

A modern data centre is an industrial site at scale: building management, power monitoring, cooling, fire and physical security, all of it OT. AI campuses add dense power and liquid cooling, and operators increasingly want AI to run parts of it. At the same time the builder works through a long chain of EPC contractors, MEP and BMS integrators and equipment vendors, and NIS2 (in Finland, the Cybersecurity Act) pushes security requirements down that whole supply chain.

Conduit pattern

AI in operations, under control

Agents that tune cooling and energy use can read BMS, EPMS and DCIM data freely, while every setpoint change passes a policy check and, where it matters, a human approval. Gains in efficiency without handing an agent the keys to the chillers.

Fathom pattern

Commissioning and handover readiness

A site self-assessment against IEC 62443 and NIS2 before handover: zone by zone, with evidence, maturity bands and a readiness brief the owner can sign off. Security becomes a measurable acceptance item, not a promise.

Conduit pattern

Contractor and vendor access

Dozens of integrators and OEMs need remote access during build and warranty. The same conduit model gives each of them scoped, time-limited and logged access, and closes it cleanly at handover.

Agent-assisted compliance

Reporting from existing data

EU energy-efficiency reporting for data centres and NIS2 evidence draw on data the site already produces. Agents can compile it; people review and sign. Less spreadsheet work, a better audit trail.

Contracting strategy

Security decided in the contract, not retrofitted after it

The cheapest moment to get OT security and AI governance right is before the contracts are signed. Each phase of a data centre project has one decision that costs little early and a great deal later.
